## SCRIPT

### Beat 0 — Hook (0:00–0:15)
**[ON SCREEN: archival street photo of the flood aftermath, slow push-in. Title card: "Boston, January 15, 1919"]**

"Most people know the joke about the Boston molasses flood. Almost nobody asks what the company did when it saw the tank leaking: it painted it brown instead of fixing it."

### Beat 1 — The turn (0:15–0:45)
**[ON SCREEN: map of Boston's North End, Commercial Street marked with a pin; harbor on the right]**

"Here is what we are going to do. We will stand on Commercial Street at lunchtime on January 15, 1919. We will look at the tank above it, at the company that built it, and at the court case that followed. By the end, you will know why this was not a joke, and why it changed how American buildings get approved."

### Beat 2 — The tank (0:45–1:50)
**[ON SCREEN: photo of the tank before the disaster; animated size comparison: tank vs. a person and a five-story building]**

"Start with the tank. It stood beside Boston Harbor, in the North End. It belonged to the Purity Distilling Company, part of United States Industrial Alcohol. It was about fifty feet tall and about ninety feet across. It was built in 1915 to hold molasses.

Not for baking. Molasses was a raw material for industrial alcohol, and in wartime, industrial alcohol meant munitions. The war had ended only two months before. And another change was about to arrive: Prohibition would be ratified the very next day."

### Beat 3 — The warnings (1:50–2:50)
**[ON SCREEN: newspaper-style text cards, one line each: "It leaked." / "They painted it brown." / "Nobody tested it." Brown paint swatch animation]**

"The tank leaked almost from the start. People who lived nearby collected the molasses that ran down its sides. The company's answer was not to repair it. According to accounts of the case, they painted the tank brown, so the leaks were harder to see.

And before it was put to work, it was not tested by filling it with water. The treasurer who oversaw the project was not an engineer. No engineer signed off on the design.

Remember that. It is the center of this story."

### Beat 4 — The afternoon (2:50–3:40)
**[ON SCREEN: thermometer graphic; clock at 12:30; sound design: a faint metallic ticking that builds]**

"January 15 was unusually warm for a Boston winter, after a hard cold. Molasses flows more easily when it warms. Around 12:30 in the afternoon, people nearby heard a sound they described as a rapid, rattling pop, like machine-gun fire. Those were the rivets giving way. Then came a deep rumble, and the tank split open."

### Beat 5 — The wave (3:40–4:40)
**[ON SCREEN: animated side-view of the wave moving down the street; labels: "~2.3 million gallons", "15–25 feet", "~35 mph"; photo of the damaged elevated rail structure and firehouse]**

"About 2.3 million gallons poured out. Witnesses and later estimates put the wave at fifteen to twenty-five feet high, moving at roughly thirty-five miles an hour. It tore a section off the elevated railway. It pushed buildings off their foundations. It knocked a firehouse off its footing.

Here is the cruel part. Molasses is heavy, and in the cold air it thickened fast. People were not just knocked down. They were trapped."

### Beat 6 — The rescue (4:40–5:20)
**[ON SCREEN: archival photo of rescuers wading through molasses; counter graphic: "21 dead. About 150 injured."]**

"Police, medics, and sailors from a Navy ship in the harbor waded in. Twenty-one people died. About one hundred and fifty were injured. The cleanup took weeks, and for months afterward people said the harbor stayed stained brown."

### Beat 7 — The blame and the payoff (5:20–6:00)
**[ON SCREEN: headline: "ANARCHISTS?"; then a court document close-up; text: "1925: the court disagrees"]**

"Now, what I promised at the start: the company saw the leaks and painted over them. And when the tank burst, its first move was to blame anarchists. This was 1919, a year of real fear about radicals and bombs. The company claimed someone had blown the tank up. It was convenient. A bomb meant it was not the company's fault.

The courts did not buy it. After years of testimony, a court-appointed auditor ruled in 1925 that the tank was not built strongly enough, and the company paid for it. This became one of the early large class-action cases in the country."

### Beat 8 — The close (6:00–6:10)
**[ON SCREEN: modern-day photo of the site; end card with two suggested videos]**

"Historians often point to this case as one reason engineers and architects now have to sign off on big structures. Not a funny story at all. If you want me to cover another forgotten American story next, tell me which one in the comments."
